**Pieza 1** · **id:** blog-01 · **canal:** blog · **fecha:** 2026-09-15
**titulo:** Three Levels, One Budget, No Criterion

**cuerpo:**

Your multilingual content budget is probably split the same way across every market: evenly. Not because anyone studied the markets and concluded they deserve equal weight, but because evenly is the version nobody has to defend in a budget review.

That evenness is not neutral. Translating a page costs less than transcreating it, and transcreating costs less than creating one from the ground up. Split the same budget evenly across every market and some of them end up over-adapted for what they needed, while others end up under-adapted for a market that looks nothing like the one the content originated in.

The split stays even because the level of adaptation gets treated as a property of the content itself: this page is complex, so it needs transcreation; this page is simple, so translation will do. Read that way, the decision belongs to a judgment about the text, not a judgment about the market — and a judgment about text cannot tell you why one market should not get the same treatment as another.

Native Reach™ already names the actual inputs to that decision. The InRegion pillar states that campaigns should match local demand, local rivals, and local buyer needs — and those three are also the three questions that decide how far a market's content should move from translation toward creation:

- Are local competitors already speaking to this market as locals, on their own terms?
- Does the conversation in this market run on its own trends, or does it echo the market you launched from?
- Are the customer's use case, objection, and reason to buy the ones you already answer at home, or different ones entirely?

The more those answers point away from home, the higher the level a market earns — not because the copy demands it, but because the market does.

That reading has to happen before a market is assigned a level, not after. In the Native Reach™ framework, Content Intelligence comes before Content Adaptation for exactly this reason: the level a market gets is the output of reading it, not a preference carried over from last year's plan.

We already laid out what separates translation, transcreation, and creation in [an earlier piece on our blog](https://www.acme-multilingual.example/blog/). This one starts where that one stopped: not what the three levels are, but which market earns which one, and why the answer cannot be the same for all of them.

If your budget is still split the same way across every market, that split is a decision nobody actually made. Talk to us about making it on purpose.

**Pieza 2** · **id:** blog-02 · **canal:** blog · **fecha:** 2026-09-18
**titulo:** The Review Nobody Runs: Did Each Market Get the Right Level?

**cuerpo:**

Six months ago, one of your markets was assigned transcreation, and another was assigned translation. Nobody has revisited either call since, because the dashboards this quarter answer a different question: did each page perform. They do not answer whether the level assigned to each market was the right one — and those are two different questions with two different answers.

A page can perform reasonably and still be under-adapted for what the market has become. A translated product page can hold steady on its own numbers and still miss the reason a local competitor is pulling ahead, because that reason showed up in the market, not in the page's own metrics. Content Performance, the fourth stage of the Native Reach™ framework, is supposed to close the loop back into Content Intelligence — but it only closes the loop if the review asks about the market, not only about the content.

That means the quarterly review should not open with "how did the page do." It should reopen the same three questions InRegion asks in the first place: are there more local competitors talking to this market as locals than there were last cycle? Has the local conversation drifted further from your home market's, or closer to it? Has the customer's reason to buy changed? Answer those again, and the level assigned six months ago may no longer be the level the market has earned today.

That is a harder review to run than a page-performance report, because it asks the team to revisit a judgment instead of just reading a chart. It is also the only version of the review that keeps the assignment honest past the cycle it was made in.

We laid out the criterion for that first assignment — rivals, demand, and buyer needs — earlier this week. The review that follows it should ask the same three questions on a schedule, instead of assuming the read from six months ago still holds.

Ready to put a review cadence behind your markets, not just a page-performance report?
