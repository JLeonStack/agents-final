### `blog-01` · Blog · 2026-09-15

**How to Decide Which Markets Get Translation, Transcreation, or Creation**

If your content budget is split evenly across your markets, that split is a decision nobody made.

It is what is left when there is no rule for deciding anything else, and it holds until someone asks why a market you have served for years and a market you opened this year are getting the same treatment. "It seemed fair" is not an answer you can take into a budget review.

## An even split is not a neutral split

Spreading the same effort across every market spends alike in two places that are not alike: one where a careful translation would have carried the message, and one where the buyer's reason to buy is not the reason it is at home. The first market is over-served. The second is under-served. The split cannot tell you which is which, and neither can the invoice: [DATO FALTANTE: costo o precio relativo de translation vs. transcreation vs. creation - el sitio no publica pricing].

## The level is a decision about the market, not about the text

Most conversations about adaptation put the subject in the wrong place: this page needs transcreation, that campaign needs creation. Pages do not need levels. Markets justify them. We defined the three levels in March, in "Guided Translation, Transcreation, and Creation: Understanding the Difference"; this is the question that comes after it, which is which market has earned which one.

Move the subject to the market and the decision acquires inputs. They are already written down, in the pillar that governs it.

## Three questions, asked per market

InRegion says campaigns "should match local demand, local rivals, and local buyer needs." For this decision that line is not a principle. It is the input list.

- **Competitors.** Does the market already have local players speaking to it as locals? The more it does, the higher the level you need to meet them on their ground.
- **Trends.** Does the conversation there run on its own topics, or echo the ones at home? The more it runs on its own, the less a translated home message has to say.
- **Customer needs.** Are the use case, the objection and the reason to buy the same as at home? The further they drift, the further up you go.

Two markets can share a language and still answer those three differently. That is the point. Language tells you what to write in; the market tells you how far to go.

## Where the decision belongs

In the Native Reach framework, Content Intelligence comes before Content Adaptation. That order is not housekeeping, it is the criterion: the level is the output of reading the market, not something you carry in. If the level was set before anyone read anything, because it is what that market got last year or what was left in the budget, the framework was run backwards.

You do not need a scoring model, and there is no honest one to give you. You need three questions, asked market by market, answered in writing, before the work is scoped. That is a split you can defend: not equal, but explainable, one market at a time.

### `blog-02` · Blog · 2026-09-17

**How to Review Content Adaptation Levels, Market by Market**

An allocation survives the meeting where it is proposed. It gets tested in the one after that, when someone asks how you know it was right.

That question comes with a wrong answer already attached: compare the markets. Line the conversion rates up, find the market at the bottom, conclude it was under-adapted. But markets differ in size, in maturity, in how crowded they are and in how long you have been in them. Ranking markets against each other measures the markets. It does not measure your decision, and there is no external number to fall back on either: [DATO FALTANTE: retorno comparado de un reparto uniforme vs. uno diferenciado por mercado - no publicado].

## Each market is compared with itself

The only defensible baseline for a market is that same market before the change: its own numbers, its own trajectory, its own period-over-period movement. That comparison answers a narrow question, and narrow is the right size here. Did more adaptation in this market change what this market does?

## What you are reviewing is the reading, not the level

When a market underperforms after you moved it up to creation, the culprit is rarely the creation work. It is usually the reading that sent it there: a competitive picture thinner than it looked from headquarters, or a set of local customer needs closer to the home market than the first assessment suggested.

So the review re-runs the three inputs the decision was made with, which are the ones InRegion names: local rivals, local demand, local buyer needs. Then it asks one question. Has the distance between this market and the home market changed, or did we misjudge it the first time? Those are different corrections. The first changes the level going forward. The second changes how the assessment gets made, and by whom.

## Markets move, and the level should move with them

A market with no strong local voice last year may have several today. A conversation that echoed the home market may have found its own subjects. Neither shows up in a content report, and both change what the right level is. Put the re-read on a fixed cadence and the level becomes something you revise on purpose, instead of something you inherit until it is indefensible.

## This is what the last stage of the framework is for

In Native Reach, Content Performance is not the end of the line. The framework runs as a continuous cycle: performance feeds back into Content Intelligence, and Content Intelligence is where the level for the next cycle gets set. A review that ends in a report has ended in the wrong place. It should end in a revised allocation.

You will not find an industry benchmark for this, and a number invented for the slide is worse than no number. What you can build instead is a record: the reading you made per market, the level it produced, what the market did next. After a few cycles that record is the argument, and it is yours.
